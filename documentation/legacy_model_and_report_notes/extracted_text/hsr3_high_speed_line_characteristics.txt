
3. Measuring Operating Costs for High Speed Rail in California







Prior to estimate the total operating cost of the Los Angeles-San Francisco high-speed rail system, the number of trains-kilometers and trainsets-kilometers as well as the number of trains-hours and trainsets-hours must be estimated. Those quantities cannot be expressed by a simple algebraic function of the level of travel demand since they depend upon numerous factors such as the fluctuations of the demand within the day and the week for every origin-destination (OD) considered in the studied network as well as the schedules, the capacity of the trainsets and the stopping pattern of the different services. SIMEX, designed by the French Railroad, is a simulation program which allows to go from the level of travel demand to the number of train(set)s-kilometers and train(set)s-hours.


3.1 The SIMEX Simulation Program

SIMEX enables one to measure and optimize operating cost for a given set of OD markets. The SIMEX simulation program provides a very detailed estimate of the operating cost and the interest and depreciation of the rolling stock. It also provides the optimal train schedules and the expected mean load factor and revenue for each train. 

In the SIMEX simulation program, the travel demand is previously estimated using the M.A.T.I.S.S.E. model designed by INRETS. The M.A.T.I.S.S.E. model also provides estimates of passengers’ time targets within the day as well as the variation of the total  demand within the week. SIMEX considers four time target distributions depending on the travel time of the OD market being studied, as shown in Appendix A. These distributions are based on the actual fluctuations of the demand observed for four French domestic OD markets : Paris-Le Mans (50 minutes), Paris-Lyon (2 hours), Paris-Bordeaux (3 hours) and Paris-Marseille (4 hours and 40 minutes). Those distributions vary whether the program is run for a random weekday or a weekly peak day (such as Friday evening or Sunday evening). The time target distributions are expressed in terms of hourly percentage of the daily demand. Every OD market is characterized by a travel time and the corresponding time target distribution.

Obviously, supply characteristics affect the travel demand. Thus, the optimal supply proposed by SIMEX must be consistent with the volume of travel demand on which the simulation is based. An algorithm, in which the M.A.T.I.S.S.E. demand model and the SIMEX simulation program are sequentially used, is run until the optimal supply proposed by SIMEX corresponds to the level of demand predicted by M.A.T.I.S.S.E.. 

The simulation program developed for the Californian corridor is based on an approach similar to the SIMEX methodology. Since M.A.T.I.S.S.E. forecasts are not available for the Californian high speed rail project, the CalSpeed travel demand estimates, as listed in Table 3.1, will be used.

3.3 Supply Characteristics

Assume that the capacity of the trainsets used for the Los Angeles-San Francisco high speed line is 350 seats. The number of services needed to carry the traffic expected to run on the new infrastructure is calculated based on the commercial capacity rather than the maximal capacity. The commercial capacity is less than the maximal capacity in order to take into account the normal fluctuations of the demand. It is assumed that the commercial capacity is 75% of the maximal capacity.

Moreover, it is assumed that the factor load on a segment must be less than 90% so that the number of available seats will be high enough to take into account the normal fluctuations of the demand. The factor load on a given segment is often lower bounded in order avoid over-capacity and, therefore, a great number of seat-miles unoccupied for a given train. Nonetheless, at this stage of the simulation, this lower bound will not be taken into account. Finally, the mean daily factor load for both express and non-stop services must be greater than 65%.

3.5  Step 2 : Non-Stop Service Scheduling Process

During off peak hours, the total demand for an express train, serving all the secondary OD markets as well as the Los Angeles-San Francisco market, may be high enough to provide a viable service whereas the travel demand for a non-stop service only serving a single OD market may be to low. During peak periods, though, the disparity of the demand among the different segments of the new line, as shown in Figure 3.2, is likely to yield empty seats on the less heavily trafficked segments. To illustrate, suppose that the Los Angeles-Bakersfield travel demand for a train leaving at 6 A.M. is so high that many passengers willing to travel beyond Bakersfield cannot get a seat. The factor load is likely to be low on the Bakersfield-Fresno segment if the number of passengers expected to get on the train in Bakersfield is negligible compared to the number of those who get off at this station.

Therefore, non-stop services should first be scheduled during peak hours so that the Los Angeles-San Francisco travel demand will be high enough to viably provide such a service. In addition, this is expected to smooth the demand allocated to express services and, thus, alleviate the problem of the disparity of the traffic volume expected on the different segments of the new line.

The non-stop service scheduling methodology used in the simulation program is pictured in Figure 3.3. The first non-stop service is scheduled at the time when the travel demand between Los Angeles and San Francisco is the highest. As shown in Figure 3.3, a train leaving Los Angeles at 6.30 A.M. would be expected to attract the greatest number of passengers. Since the maximum load factor is 90%, only 315 passengers are allocated to this train. Scheduling the 6.30 A.M. train affects the potential demand for non-stop services leaving Los Angeles between 6 A.M. and 7 A.M. as shown in Figure 3.4. The  new demand profile leads us to schedule the next train at 3.15 P.M.. The remaining demand after scheduling the 6.30 A.M. and the 3.15 P.M. trains is pictured in Figure 3.5. It leads us to schedule the next trains at 4 P.M., 2.30 P.M. and 9.15 A.M. as shown in Figure 3.5. The same methodology is used to schedule the remaining Los Angeles-San Francisco non-stop services. 3.6  Step 3 : Express Service Scheduling Process


Every fifteen minutes, from 5 A.M. to 12 A.M., the potential demand for an express service is calculated. If the potential demand for a given express service on the most heavily trafficked segment exceeds 90% of the capacity, the demand on this segment is truncated so that the number of passengers potentially attracted by this service is exactly 90% of the capacity. The composition of the travel demand according to the different OD markets remains the same. To illustrate, suppose that the travel demand for an express service leaving Los Angeles at 8.30 A.M. is 370 passengers on the Los Angeles-Bakersfield segment while the capacity is 350 seats. Only 315 passengers can be allocated to this service. Assume that 35% of these 370 passengers belong to the Los Angeles-San Jose OD market, 35% of the 315 passengers actually allocated to the 8.30 A.M. express service will travel from Los Angeles to San Jose. This new demand allocation has an impact on the other segments served by the express train. If the demand on the most heavily trafficked remaining segment exceeds 90%, the same operation is repeated. The final travel demand allocation is completed as when the factor loads in the different segments of the line is less than 90%. 

The Los Angeles-San Francisco travel demand is allocated to the different express services assuming that the average period of time during which an express train is expected to attract Los Angeles-San Francisco passengers is 2 minutes as discussed in section 3.3. Thus, the conditional probability that an express train attract Los Angeles-San Francisco passengers knowing the non-stop schedules is not taken into account at this stage of the simulation.

Then, knowing the composition of the demand according to the different OD markets, it is possible to calculate the expected revenue for the studied service. 3.8 Operating Cost Estimates


The simulation provided estimates of the expected number of passengers carried as well as the number of trains or trainsets-kilometers and train or trainsets-hours. In order to estimate the interest and depreciation of the rolling stock, the number of trainsets required to provide the 108 services serving the Los Angeles-San Francisco corridor must be calculated. As shown in Appendix B, the required number of trainsets is 34. This minimum number is usually increased by 5% in order to take into account the non-disposability a proportion of the total fleet due to defection or maintenance. Thus the total number of trainsets in the fleet would be 36.
