Chapter 6:   Full Cost of High Speed Rail
6.0 INTRODUCTION

High speed rail systems are operational in Japan, France, and Germany, serving millions of passengers per year.  However, these systems are not free.  They have costs associated with building and maintaining the infrastructure and operating trains and controls systems.  These costs are dependent upon passenger demand.  Furthermore, high speed rails systems have a noticeable noise externality, which must be considered.  Last, travelers commit the precious commodity of time when traveling.
The method used to estimate the full cost (FC) of high speed travel will combine elements from a number of sources.  Broadly, these include User Time Costs (UC), Infrastructure Costs (IC), Carrier Vehicle Costs (CVC), Carrier Operating Costs (COC), Environmental Costs (SEC), Congestion Costs (STC), Noise Costs (SNC), and Accident and Safety Costs  (SAC).  Because of the safety rates of the existing high speed rail systems, we will assume no risk of accident.  This does not mean there is no safety cost, rather that it is incorporated in higher capital costs to design the system to be safer.  We assume the system has sufficient capacity that congestion does not occur.  Also, because these systems are electrically powered, we assume that there are no environmental externalities caused by the rail system, and that the cost of pollution is internalized in the electric generation sector of the economy. Each of these costs is a function of various parameters, which may include usage of the system.  Thus the full cost in many ways depends upon the demand.  This chapter provides both the function and a range of point estimates based upon assumptions of demand and other factors.
This chapter will evaluate in turn the the infrastructure costs, the carrier operating and capital costs of the system, and the social costs of high speed rail travel and the cost of time to users.  The final section will combine the costs to develop a cost table for high speed rail.

6.2. The Structure of High Speed Rail Carrier Costs
A study conducted by INRETS and INTRAPLAN provided estimates of the average high speed rail operating cost for Europe. In this study, operating costs were divided into the following breakdown :
6.2.1 Sales and Administration
Sales and administration costs include labor costs for ticket sales and for providing information at the railroad stations. They also include costs for automated ticketing machine  and travel agency commissions. In the INRETS/INTRAPLAN study, sales and administration costs have not been estimated on the basis of the required number of staff and automated ticketing machine for a given level of expected traffic volume but have been assumed to represent 10% of the passenger revenue.
6.2.2 Shunting
Shunting costs depend on the distance between the depot and the station as well as the average period of time trainsets stay at the depot. Nonetheless, to simplify, shunting costs could be approximate on a per train basis. The study conducted by INRETS and INTRAPLAN has shown that the cost of labor represents 80% of the total shunting cost.
6.2.3 Train Operations
Train operation can be divided into four activities :
train servicing,
driving,
operations and safety on high speed lines, and
operations and safety on conventional lines.

Train operation costs consist exclusively in labor costs. Train servicing and driving for the South-East TGV and the Atlantic TGV is two train companions per trainset and one driver per train (which may include one or two trainsets). Operations and safety on either high speed or conventional lines can be estimated on a per train basis.
6.2.4 Maintenance of Way and Equipment
The cost of the maintenance of electric traction installations and catenary depends on the number of trains running on the infrastructure whereas the cost of maintaining the tracks depends on the number of trainsets. Theoretically, the cost of maintenance of equipment is dependent upon the distance run by every trainset as well as the duration of use. In the INRETS / INTRAPLAN study, the impact of the duration of use has been ignored so that maintenance of equipment cost can be estimated on a trainset per kilometer basis.
According to the INRETS / INTRAPLAN study, the proportions of the cost of labor in the maintenance costs are 55% for maintenance of electric traction installations, 45% for maintenance of tracks and 50% for maintenance of equipment.
6.2.5 Energy
Costs can be estimated from the average consumption of energy required per kilometer which characterized the trainsets. The cost of energy is assumed to take into account the cost of transport and the electrical losses between the power generating station and the substations. Operating costs related to energy do not include any labor cost.
Tables 6.3 and 6.4 give the average energy consumption for the South-East TGV and the Atlantic TGV running and the new infrastructure and upgraded lines, respectively, for a load factor of 65%. Energy consumption per passenger varies with the speed and increases rapidly when the speed is over 300 kph (Pavaux, 1991).

6.3.	Measuring Operating Costs for High Speed Rail in California

Unitary operating costs are expected to differ between California and Europe, especially when labour cost represents a significant percentage of the total unitary cost. Nonetheless, since there is no currently operating high speed rail system in California or elsewhere in the United States,  it would be difficult to estimate specific unitary costs for California. Thus, INRETS / INTRAPLAN estimates will be used to forecast the operating cost of high speed rail in California except for energy and sales and administration costs.
Sales and administration costs are dependent on the required number of staff and automated ticketing machine for a given level of expected traffic volume. Assuming that they represent 10% of the passenger revenue in California would imply that the revenue per passenger would be comparable to those observed in Europe. Thus, it may be more accurate to estimate salse and administration costs on a per passenger basis rather than revenue. As a first approximation, sales and administration costs in California will be assumed to be $ 5 per passenger.
The unit cost rate for electrical power pricing in this analysis will be assumed to be $0.10 per kilowatt-hour, implicitly assuming full cost pricing within the electrical generation sector. According to Table 6.3, the energy consumption of a Atlantic TGV trainset cruising at 300 kilometers per hour on the new high speed line is 20 kilowatt-hour per kilometer. The maximum speed on the California high speed line has been assumed to be 200 mph (320 kph). Moreover, the average number of train stops on the new line  is expected to be higher. Thus, the energy consumption on the new line for California will be assumed to be 25 kilowatt-hour per kilometer and per trainset. It is assumed that there is now conventional line segment between Los Angeles and San Francisco.
6.3.1	The SIMEX Simulation Program

In order to estimate the total operating cost of the Los Angeles-San Francisco high-speed rail system, the number of trains-kilometers and trainsets-kilometers as well as the number of trains-hours and trainsets-hours must be calculated. Those quantities cannot be expressed by a simple algebraic function of the level of travel demand since they depend upon numerous factors such as the fluctuations of the demand within the day and the week for every origin-destination (OD) considered in the studied network as well as the schedules, the capacity of the trainsets and the stopping pattern of the different services. SIMEX, designed by the French Railroad, is a simulation program which translates from the level of travel demand to the number of train(set)s-kilometers and train(set)s-hours.
SIMEX enables one to measure and optimize operating cost for a given set of OD markets. The SIMEX simulation program provides a very detailed estimate of the operating cost and the number of trainsets required to supply services. It also provides the optimal train schedules and the expected mean load factor and revenue for each train. 
In the SIMEX simulation program, the travel demand is previously estimated using the M.A.T.I.S.S.E. model designed by INRETS. The M.A.T.I.S.S.E. model also provides estimates of passengers’ time targets within the day as well as the variation of the total  demand within the week. SIMEX considers four time target distributions depending on the travel time of the OD market being studied, as shown in Appendix 6-B. These distributions are based on the actual fluctuations of the demand observed for four French domestic OD markets : Paris-Le Mans (50 minutes), Paris-Lyon (2 hours), Paris-Bordeaux (3 hours) and Paris-Marseille (4 hours and 40 minutes). Those distributions vary whether the program is run for a random weekday or a weekly peak day (such as Friday evening or Sunday evening). The time target distributions are expressed in terms of hourly percentage of the daily demand. Every OD market is characterized by a travel time and the corresponding time target distribution.
Obviously, supply characteristics affect the travel demand. Thus, the optimal supply proposed by SIMEX must be consistent with the volume of travel demand on which the simulation is based. An algorithm, in which the M.A.T.I.S.S.E. demand model and the SIMEX simulation program are sequentially used, is run until the optimal supply proposed by SIMEX corresponds to the level of demand predicted by M.A.T.I.S.S.E.. 
The simulation program developed for the Californian corridor is based on an approach similar to the SIMEX methodology. Since M.A.T.I.S.S.E. forecasts are not available for the Californian high speed rail project, the CalSpeed travel demand estimates, as listed in Table 6.7, will be used.

6.3.3	Supply Characteristics

Assume that the capacity of the trainsets used for the Los Angeles-San Francisco high speed line is 350 seats. The number of services needed to carry the traffic expected to run on the new infrastructure is calculated based on the commercial capacity rather than the maximal capacity. The commercial capacity is less than the maximal capacity in order to take into account the normal fluctuations of the demand. It is assumed that the commercial capacity is 75% of the maximal capacity.
Moreover, it is assumed that the factor load on a segment must be less than 90% so that the number of available seats will be high enough to take into account the normal fluctuations of the demand. The factor load on a given segment is often lower bounded in order avoid over-capacity and, therefore, a great number of seat-miles unoccupied for a given train. Nonetheless, at this stage of the simulation, this lower bound will not be taken into account. Finally, the mean daily factor load for both express and non-stop services must be greater than 65%.

6.3.3.2	Step 2 : Non-Stop Service Scheduling Process

During off peak hours, the total demand for an express train, serving all the secondary OD markets as well as the Los Angeles-San Francisco market, may be high enough to provide a viable service whereas the travel demand for a non-stop service only serving a single OD market may be to low. During peak periods, though, the disparity of the demand among the different segments of the new line, as shown in Figure 6.3, is likely to yield empty seats on the less heavily trafficked segments. To illustrate, suppose that the Los Angeles-Bakersfield travel demand for a train leaving at 6 A.M. is so high that many passengers willing to travel beyond Bakersfield cannot get a seat. The factor load is likely to be low on the Bakersfield-Fresno segment if the number of passengers expected to get on the train in Bakersfield is negligible compared to the number of those who get off at this station.
Therefore, non-stop services should first be scheduled during peak hours so that the Los Angeles-San Francisco travel demand will be high enough to viably provide such a service. In addition, this is expected to smooth the demand allocated to express services and, thus, alleviate the problem of the disparity of the traffic volume expected on the different segments of the new line.

6.3.3.3	Step 3 : Express Service Scheduling Process

The express service scheduling process is somewhat more complex since it must be directly based on the maximization of the revenue rather than the potential number of passenger attracted by a given service. While non-stop service revenue is proportional to the number of passengers carried, the travel demand potentially attracted by a given express service corresponds to different OD markets and, thus, different fares. Moreover, the disparity of the demand in the different segments of the new line is likely to yield empty seats on the less heavily trafficked segments as stated before. Thus, the express service which leads to the highest revenue does not necessarily correspond to the one which attracts the greatest number of passengers.
Every fifteen minutes, from 5 A.M. to 12 A.M., the potential demand for an express service is calculated. If the potential demand for a given express service on the most heavily trafficked segment exceeds 90% of the capacity, the demand on this segment is truncated so that the number of passengers potentially attracted by this service is exactly 90% of the capacity. The composition of the travel demand according to the different OD markets remains the same. To illustrate, suppose that the travel demand for an express service leaving Los Angeles at 8.30 A.M. is 370 passengers on the Los Angeles-Bakersfield segment while the capacity is 350 seats. Only 315 passengers can be allocated to this service. Assume that 35% of these 370 passengers belong to the Los Angeles-San Jose OD market, 35% of the 315 passengers actually allocated to the 8.30 A.M. express service will travel from Los Angeles to San Jose. This new demand allocation has an impact on the other segments served by the express train. If the demand on the most heavily trafficked remaining segment exceeds 90%, the same operation is repeated. The final travel demand allocation is completed as when the factor loads in the different segments of the line is less than 90%. 
The Los Angeles-San Francisco travel demand is allocated to the different express services assuming that the average period of time during which an express train is expected to attract Los Angeles-San Francisco passengers is 2 minutes as discussed in section 6.2.3. Thus, the conditional probability that an express train attract Los Angeles-San Francisco passengers knowing the non-stop schedules is not taken into account at this stage of the simulation.
Then, knowing the composition of the demand according to the different OD markets, it is possible to calculate the expected revenue for the studied service.
Although the travel demand is assumed to be symmetric, the express service scheduling process is likely to yield different schedules and revenues. To illustrate a train leaving Los Angeles at 8.30 A.M. will attract passengers from Los Angeles to San Jose and San Francisco whose time target is closed to 8.30 A.M. while a 8.30 A.M. express service from San Francisco to Los Angeles will potentially attract San Francisco-Los Angeles passengers whose time target is closed to 8.30 A.M. as well as San Jose-Los Angeles passengers whose time target is closed to 8.57 A.M..

6.4	Social Cost and User Time Cost
6.4.1 Noise Cost
The social costs of HSR are restricted to noise. Accident costs are negligable and we assume no congestion delays. We also do not, at this point, consider pollution costs, however, this is an issue which is under debate.  With electrically powered trainunits, the pollution from power generation is moved backwards in the supply relationship. We argue that this pollution is properly associated with the electric power generation sector, in which additional pollution costs are, or should be, internalized.  Some have argued that the incremental pollution due to the increase in power requirements from the public utility which supplies power to the HSR should be included as part of the social costs of HSR, because it represents an avoidable cost. 
The basis for estimating noise costs for HSR are provided in detail in Chapter 3. The complete model is complex, requiring the combination of a number of equations.  For analytical purposes, this was converted to a simpler average cost ($/pkt) model.  A regression was performed after fixing a number of assumptions discussed in Chapter 3, with the independent variable being the number of trains per hour (QT) valid for the range from 0-10 trains per hour, and the dependent variable being $/pkt .

The social cost can be calculated for various train speeds as a function of the number of trains per hour. We illustrate by computing the marginal social cost for two representative speeds, 160 km/hr and 300 km/hr.  As with the calculation of the air noise costs there are key assumptions regarding housing density, housing values and cost per dB.  Analogous with the valuation of the air and highway noise externality, valuation is based on a hedonic methodology. The equations are provided below.
MCtn300 = 0.0819 -0.0428(ln (QT)) + 0.000294 * QT2

At 160 km/hr, our best estimate of the expected cost of noise is $0.017 per passenger kilometer ($0.028/pmt); at 300 km/hr it is $0.020/pkt ($0.033/pmt), assuming 5 trains per hour and 200 passengers per train. 
6.4.2  User Time Cost
User cost of time depends on the speed of service.  If we assume the system operates at 160 km/hr, and the value of time is $10/hour, then we have a $0.0625/pkt user time cost. At 300 km/hr, then the cost of time is $0.033/pkt.  This suggests, that for a 1000 km trip, the user is contributing $33.33 in his time toward the trip.
